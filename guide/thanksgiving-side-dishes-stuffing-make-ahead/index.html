Thanksgiving Side Dishes 2026: Stuffing, Make-Ahead Plan and Food Safety

Published October 25, 2026 · GiveOwl

The turkey gets the headlines, but most make-ahead decisions are about the sides. This page covers what the USDA says about stuffing (or dressing), casseroles and cold dishes, a day-by-day plan around 2026 Thanksgiving, and two simple recipes.

Stuffing, filling or dressing?

The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) says the dish is called stuffing, filling or dressing depending on the region, and it is usually based on a bread mixture. Because stuffing is "an excellent medium for bacterial growth", FSIS asks you to cook it to a safe minimum internal temperature, measured with a food thermometer. FSIS also advises cooking stuffing separately from the turkey for optimal safety and even cooking.

Making stuffing ahead: the FSIS rules

  • Prepare the parts, not the whole. The dry and wet ingredients can be prepared ahead and chilled, but should not be mixed until just before the stuffing goes into the oven.
  • Keep it moist. Stuffing should be moist, not dry, because heat destroys bacteria more rapidly in a moist environment.
  • Straight into the oven. Put stuffing in an oven set no lower than 325°F immediately after mixing, and check with a food thermometer that it reaches 165°F.
  • Do not refrigerate uncooked stuffing. If you assemble it ahead, it must be frozen or cooked immediately. To freeze, combine the ingredients, put them in a shallow container and freeze at once. Do not thaw it before cooking; cook it from frozen until it reaches 165°F.
  • Raw meat or shellfish in the mix. Precook raw meat, poultry or shellfish ingredients first. FSIS recommends cooking oysters before they go into stuffing.
  • Slow cooker stuffing. It must be very moist; fill the cooker loosely, no more than two-thirds full; use a tight lid; cook on high for at least one hour before turning it down to low; and cook until the center reaches 165°F. Never put frozen stuffing in a slow cooker. Do not reheat leftover stuffing in one either: use an oven set no lower than 325°F or a microwave.
  • Store-bought stuffing. FSIS says uncooked premixed stuffing is not safe and should not be bought; cooked, refrigerated stuffing is fine if you reheat it to 165°F.
  • Cooked stuffing. Cool it in shallow containers and refrigerate within two hours. The FSIS stuffing page says to use it within 3 to 4 days and reheat to 165°F; do not stuff a bird with leftover cooked stuffing.

Casseroles, eggs and cold dishes

  • Temperatures to hit. The FSIS chart lists 165°F for casseroles and leftovers, 165°F for stuffing and poultry, and 160°F for eggs. Check with a food thermometer before taking the dish off the heat.
  • Hot stays hot, cold stays cold. FSIS says to keep hot food at 140°F or above, for example in chafing dishes, preheated warming trays or slow cookers, and cold food at 40°F or below, for example in containers on ice.
  • The two-hour rule. Bacteria grow fastest between 40°F and 140°F and can double in as little as 20 minutes. Do not leave food out of refrigeration for more than two hours, or one hour if the temperature is above 90°F.
  • Keep sides away from raw turkey. FSIS says to keep raw turkey separate from foods that will not be cooked later, such as vegetables, fruit and side dishes, and to wash cutting boards, utensils and counters after handling raw turkey.
  • Leftovers. FSIS says to store leftovers, including side dishes, within two hours of cooking in covered shallow containers, and to eat them within four days or by the Monday after Thanksgiving, or freeze them. Reheat to 165°F.

A make-ahead plan for 2026

Our own plan, built only from the FSIS rules above:

  • Wednesday, November 25: chop the vegetables and dry the bread cubes. Chill the dry and wet parts of the stuffing in separate containers; do not mix them. If you want to freeze a finished stuffing, assemble it, put it in a shallow container and freeze it immediately. Make the cranberry sauce and refrigerate it.
  • Thursday, November 26: mix the stuffing just before it goes into the oven and bake it straight away; if frozen, do not thaw it. Check 165°F in the center. Put cold dishes out on ice and keep hot dishes at 140°F or above. Start the two-hour clock when food comes to the table, and refrigerate leftovers within that time.
  • Friday, November 27: reheat leftovers to 165°F before eating.
  • Monday, November 30: the Monday after Thanksgiving, by when refrigerated leftovers should be eaten or frozen (FSIS).

Simple baked stuffing

Makes about 10 cups. FSIS suggests allowing at least ½ cup per person when stuffing is baked in a casserole.

Ingredients

  • 12 cups (about 1 lb) day-old bread, cut into ¾-inch cubes
  • ½ cup (1 stick) butter, plus a little for the dish
  • 1 large onion and 3 celery stalks, chopped
  • 2 teaspoons dried sage, 1 teaspoon dried thyme, 1 teaspoon salt and ½ teaspoon black pepper
  • 2 to 2½ cups chicken or vegetable broth
  • 2 large eggs, beaten

Method

  1. Spread the bread cubes on two baking sheets and dry them in a 250°F oven for about 30 minutes. Let them cool.
  2. Melt the butter in a large pan and cook the onion and celery for 8 to 10 minutes until soft. Stir in the herbs, salt and pepper. If you are cooking ahead, cool this mixture and chill it separately from the bread.
  3. Just before baking, heat the oven to 350°F and butter a 9 × 13-inch baking dish.
  4. In a large bowl, toss the bread with the vegetables. Stir the broth and eggs together and mix them in, adding the broth gradually until the stuffing is moist but not soggy.
  5. Spoon it loosely into the dish and cover with foil. Bake for 25 minutes, then uncover and bake for another 20 to 25 minutes, until the top is browned and the center reads 165°F on a food thermometer.
  6. Serve hot. Refrigerate leftovers within two hours.

This is our own simple recipe with everyday ingredients; we have not kitchen-tested it, so treat times and temperatures as a guide and adjust for your oven. Allergens: it contains wheat (bread), milk (butter), eggs and celery, and broth can contain other allergens, so read the labels before you cook for others. Use vegetable broth for a vegetarian version.

Simple cranberry sauce

A cold side dish. Makes about 2 cups.

  • 12 oz (one bag) fresh cranberries
  • 1 cup granulated sugar
  • 1 cup water
  1. Stir the sugar and water in a saucepan over medium heat until the sugar dissolves, then bring to a boil.
  2. Add the cranberries and simmer for about 10 minutes, until most of the berries have burst.
  3. Cool, then refrigerate in a covered container. It thickens as it cools.
  4. Serve cold. On a buffet, keep it at 40°F or below, for example in a bowl set in ice, and follow the two-hour rule.

Our own recipe, not kitchen-tested; the sauce contains none of the major allergens, but check the label of the cranberries and sugar if you cook for someone with allergies.
