How sweepstakes work in the US

A sweepstakes gives prizes away by chance, and you do not have to buy anything to take part. The US Postal Service puts it this way: “You never have to purchase an item or pay a fee to enter and win a sweepstakes” (USPS Publication 546, via the Postal Inspection Service).

Some states have their own rules for sponsors. In New York, a chance promotion tied to selling products or services with more than $5,000 in prizes must be filed with the Secretary of State at least 30 days before it starts, and the sponsor must hold a trust account or bond for the prize value (General Business Law § 369-e). In Florida, a game promotion with more than $5,000 in prizes must be filed with the Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services at least 7 days before it starts, also with a trust account or surety bond (Florida Statutes § 849.094). Check each sponsor’s official rules for who can enter.

Prizes count as income. The IRS says: “If you win a prize in a lucky number drawing, television or radio quiz program, beauty contest, or other event, you must include it in your income” (IRS Publication 525); prizes in goods or services count at their fair market value. This is general information, not legal or tax advice.

Is it legal to enter sweepstakes in the US?

Yes. A sweepstakes must give you a free way to enter: USPS Publication 546 says you never have to buy anything or pay a fee to enter and win. Check each sponsor's official rules for who can enter.

Do some states have their own sweepstakes rules?

Yes. New York (General Business Law § 369-e) and Florida (Florida Statutes § 849.094) require sponsors of chance promotions tied to selling products or services with more than $5,000 in prizes to file before the start (30 days in New York, 7 days in Florida) and to secure the prize value with a bond or trust account.

Do I have to pay tax on a sweepstakes prize?

Prizes are taxable income in the US. IRS Publication 525 says a prize must be included in your income, and a prize in goods or services counts at its fair market value. This is general information, not tax advice.
